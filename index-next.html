The orchestration gap

One layer of the robotics stack has no neutral standard

Application programming, simulation, and the hardware and model layers each have well-backed contenders. Fleet orchestration does not — and it is the layer that decides whether a mixed fleet can be operated as one fleet.

Vanceda builds operator software on the orchestration layer. The layer itself is an open standard — Apache 2.0, developed in the open, and free for anyone including our competitors to implement.

How it shows up

Three operations, one problem

The pattern is the same wherever a facility has bought robots from more than one supplier: capability arrives per vendor, and coordination does not arrive at all.

Warehouse

The problem
Autonomous mobile robots from two manufacturers share one floor. Each has its own console and its own traffic rules, and neither knows the other exists.
What the software does
Both fleets appear as one fleet. Zone-based deconfliction applies across vendors, and tasks are assigned on capability rather than on which console you happen to have open.
What the operator gets
One person runs the floor instead of two, and adding a third vendor does not add a third console.

Hospital

The problem
Delivery robots, lifts and doors come from different suppliers. When something stops, safety and compliance need evidence that it stopped — not an assurance.
What the software does
Stop commands are confirmed on the robot and recorded, with tamper-evident event records and signature-verified firmware and model updates.
What the operator gets
An auditable record for a safety review, produced by the system rather than reconstructed afterwards.

Manufacturing

The problem
Every new robot model means a new integration project, written against one vendor's API that changes on their schedule.
What the software does
Vendors implement one open adapter contract, verified by a published conformance suite, rather than being integrated one at a time.
What the operator gets
A new vendor is a procurement decision, not an engineering project.

Built on

Technology

The edge dependency is written into a public normative specification rather than being aspirational.

NVIDIA Jetson · linux/arm64

The specification normatively places production fleet adapters and the edge zone controller on Jetson hardware. The edge node is a compiled Go binary targeting arm64 and runs the safety-critical stop path independently of the cloud.

ROS 2 · Isaac ROS

A conformant ROS 2 reference fleet adapter, integrating with Nav2 for navigation actions.

Isaac Sim

Target environment for visual fleet simulation — a warehouse scene with multiple robots demonstrating hardware-fault-driven task reassignment.

cuOpt

Under evaluation as a routing and assignment optimiser behind the zone-based traffic deconfliction engine.

Provable, not asserted

Safety and provenance

In a hospital, a defence facility or a regulated plant, "the robot stopped" is not a claim you make — it is a claim you evidence. That constraint shapes the architecture rather than sitting on top of it.

Confirmed stop paths

A stop is acknowledged by the robot and recorded, and the safety path runs on an edge node independently of the cloud control plane. Losing the network does not lose the ability to stop.

Tamper-evident event records

Fleet events are recorded so that alteration is detectable, which is what turns an operations log into evidence a reviewer can rely on.

Signature-verified updates

Firmware and model updates are signature-verified before they reach a machine, so the supply chain into the fleet is as auditable as the fleet itself.

The standard underneath

Built on Swarmada

Vanceda builds on Swarmada, an open standard for robot fleet orchestration released under Apache 2.0.

Swarmada is developed in the open with its own governance and is free for anyone to use, implement or fork.

Vanceda holds no special control over the project and uses it on the same terms as any other adopter. Our products sit above the standard; the standard itself is not ours to own.

Vanceda's own products are commercial and proprietary. The standard they run on is neither, and that separation is deliberate — it is what makes the layer safe for a competitor to adopt and safe for a customer to depend on.

  • Direct influence over the specification while it is still being written

  • Engineering attention a later customer will not get

What we need from you

  • A real mixed fleet, even a small one
  • An operations contact who will tell us when it is wrong
  • A pilot area where a failure is inconvenient rather than dangerous

What a pilot looks like

  • Read-only fleet visibility first — no commands, no risk
  • Then task assignment in one zone, existing consoles still available
  • Then deconfliction across vendors, once the first two have earned it
